# 64 - Prioritization Methods

## You Prioritize Everything

Not just user stories — epics, initiatives, marketing campaigns, product launches. Priorities shift constantly due to competitors, new tech, team dependencies.

## Three Classic Methods

### 1. Assumption Testing

Score each item on two dimensions, then add:

| Dimension | Scale | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk** (biggest assumption) | 1-10 | How risky is our assumption? (10 = very risky) |
| **Importance** (impact) | 1-10 | How important is this to build? |
| **Priority score** | Risk + Importance | Sort highest first |

**Best for**: Stories inside an epic. "If we're wrong about this, try it first to find out."

### 2. BUC Method (Business, User, Cost)

| Factor | Score (1-10) |
|---|---|
| **B**usiness benefits | How much does the business gain? |
| **U**ser benefits | How much does the user gain? |
| **C**ost | How expensive/difficult is it? |

**Formula**: `(Business + User) - Cost = Priority Score`

Sort by highest score. Challenge: sometimes not enough data to score accurately.

### 3. MoSCoW Method

Imagine you build **nothing**. What's the worst that happens?

| Category | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Must** | Cannot live without — business fails or breaks | Login system, payment processing |
| **Should** | Important but not fatal if missing | Email notifications |
| **Could** | Nice to have if time allows | Dark mode |
| **Would** | Someday, if we get the opportunity | Custom themes |

**Rule**: If a Must depends on something else, that dependency is also a Must.

## Beyond Methods: Real-World Factors

| Factor | How it affects priority |
|---|---|
| Competitor moves | They launch something → you react |
| New technology available | Enables something previously impossible |
| Other teams' work | Dependencies change your timeline |
| Market opportunity / timing | Window to capture market may close |
| Budget constraints | Can't do everything at once |
| Company acquisitions/bankruptcies | Entire strategies shift |

> Some of the biggest product successes came from **instinct + risk-taking**, not fancy prioritization frameworks.
